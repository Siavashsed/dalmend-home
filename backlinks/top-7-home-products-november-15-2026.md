## QUORA
**Answer this question:** Can you install an induction range in a house with a 100-amp electrical panel?

Often you can, but a standard induction range is the hardest way to do it, and you should have a licensed electrician run a load calculation before you buy anything.

A full-size induction range usually needs its own dedicated 240-volt circuit, often 40 to 50 amps. On a 100-amp service that also runs a dryer, a water heater, HVAC and everything else, there may not be enough headroom left. When the load calculation says no, the usual answer is a service upgrade. That can mean electrician fees, utility scheduling and weeks of waiting, all before the range arrives.

You have a few ways around that:

1. Load management. Smart panels or circuit-sharing devices can let a range and a dryer share capacity so they never peak at the same time. Your electrician will know what local code allows.

2. A smaller-draw cooktop. Some two-burner and 120-volt induction units exist. They work for light cooking but feel limited in a family kitchen.

3. A battery-assisted range. This is the option I've been most impressed by. The range charges a built-in battery from a modest circuit and draws on it when you cook hard, so it pulls far less from the panel.

In October I cooked on one in a client's Hudson Valley home with a 100-amp panel. I boiled a pot of pasta water while the dryer was running. It took under two minutes, nothing tripped and no electrician had to be called. These ranges cost around $6,000, which sounds steep until you price a panel upgrade next to it.
