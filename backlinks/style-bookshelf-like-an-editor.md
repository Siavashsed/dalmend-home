**Title:** Anyone else find that bookshelves look better when about a third of the space is left empty?

I've been styling shelves for a few years, and the single biggest improvement I've seen is also the least intuitive: take almost everything off, then put back only about two thirds of it.

The moment that convinced me was a townhouse job in Brooklyn. A nine-foot oak wall unit held 214 books, a ceramic owl and four dead succulents. Everything was crammed in, so nothing could be seen. We pulled it all onto the floor and started over. By the end of the day only 140 books had earned their place back, and the shelves looked calmer and richer at the same time.

A few things that helped:

- Empty space does real work. It gives the eye somewhere to rest, so the objects you keep actually get noticed.
- Group books by color or size in small clusters instead of one long row, and mix vertical stacks with horizontal ones.
- Add one or two objects per shelf, not six. A single good piece beats a crowd of small ones.
- Get rid of anything dead, dusty or sentimental-but-ugly. Be honest about it.

The hardest part is tolerating a bare patch of shelf. It feels wasteful until you step back and see it working.

How do you handle this? Do you keep your shelves full, or have you gone minimal? I'd love to hear what's worked for you.

## QUORA
**Answer this question:** How do I style a bookshelf so it looks designed rather than cluttered?

The most effective advice I can give is also the most counterintuitive: take most of it off, then put back only about two thirds and leave the rest bare.

I learned this while working in a Brooklyn townhouse, standing in front of a nine-foot oak wall unit that held 214 books, a ceramic owl and four dead succulents. Nothing on it was being seen because everything was competing. We emptied it onto the floor, and by the end of the day only 140 books had earned a place back. The result looked calmer, more expensive and far more intentional.

Here is the process I'd follow:

1. Empty the shelves completely. You can't edit what you can't see, and a full reset stops you from simply rearranging clutter.

2. Be strict about what returns. Throw out anything dead, broken or dusty. Keep books you actually love or use, and objects with a real reason to be there.

3. Leave about a third of the space empty. This is what makes a shelf feel styled. Bare space gives the eye somewhere to rest and lets the remaining pieces breathe.

4. Vary how you arrange things. Mix vertical rows with horizontal stacks, group books in small clusters by color or size, and avoid lining everything up in one long uniform row.

5. Use objects sparingly. One or two pieces per shelf, in different heights and materials, work better than a crowd of small trinkets.

6. Step back and adjust. Look at the whole unit from across the room, not just up close, and move things until the weight feels balanced.

The hardest part is emotional. A bare patch of shelf feels wasteful at first, but it's usually what turns a storage unit into a feature of the room.
