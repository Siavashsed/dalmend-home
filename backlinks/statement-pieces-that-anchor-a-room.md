**Title:** Why do some rooms full of beautiful pieces still feel chaotic?

I've been thinking about why some rooms feel calm and others feel busy, even when every piece in them is gorgeous. My current theory is that the busy rooms rarely lack nice things. They usually have too many nice things competing for attention.

A room seems to settle when one piece is allowed to be the loudest thing in it and everything else agrees to be quieter. Picture a bold patterned sofa, a dramatic chandelier, a sculptural coffee table and a big art piece all at the same volume. Your eye has nowhere to land, so the room reads as scattered.

What's worked for me: pick the one piece you'd save in a fire, then check each other item. Does it support that piece or argue with it? Supporting pieces can still be beautiful. They just pull back on color, pattern or scale. Sometimes that means moving a favorite to another room instead of getting rid of it.

Does anyone else do this on purpose? Or have you seen rooms where two statement pieces actually get along?

## QUORA
**Answer this question:** How do I make my living room feel cohesive when I own several bold furniture pieces?

I'm a decor editor and stylist, and this is the most common problem I see in well-furnished homes. The room rarely lacks beautiful things. It usually has four or five of them, and they are all competing for attention.

The principle I work from: a room feels anchored when one piece is allowed to be the loudest thing in it and everything else agrees to be quieter.

Here is how I apply it:

1. Choose your lead. Pick the one piece the room should be remembered for. It might be a patterned sofa, a dramatic light fixture or a sculptural table. Usually it's the piece you'd be most upset to lose.

2. Turn the others down. Supporting pieces don't need to be boring, but each should give way on at least one thing: color, pattern, scale or shine. A bold print sofa works better with a simple rug than with a second bold pattern.

3. Use placement. The lead piece gets the sightline from the doorway and the best light. Secondary pieces sit off to the side or lower down.

4. Relocate, don't discard. If two pieces truly clash, one of them often does well in a hallway, bedroom or dining room where it can lead on its own.

5. Edit accessories last. Small objects add noise fast. Once the lead is clear, remove anything that repeats its job.

Last spring I worked on a Toronto living room with a 1970s travertine coffee table, a smoky Murano chandelier and a sofa in a bold Pierre Frey print. Each piece was stunning, and together they were exhausting. Deciding which one would lead changed the room more than any purchase could have.
